# Camera Techniques: Research Guide

**Date:** 2026-10-11  
**Scope:** A practical taxonomy of techniques used in still photography and filmmaking. “All” is not a closed list: techniques overlap, vary by production, and new tools continue to appear.

## 1. Framing and shot size

Shot size describes how much of the subject and surroundings appear in the frame. Names are approximate and vary with lens, subject distance, and convention.

| Technique | What it shows / common purpose |
|---|---|
| Extreme wide / extreme long shot | Subject is tiny or absent; establishes scale, place, or isolation. |
| Wide / long shot | Whole subject and substantial surroundings; shows geography or physical action. |
| Full shot | A person from head to toe; keeps movement and body language visible. |
| Medium-long / three-quarter shot | Roughly knees or thighs upward; combines gesture and context. |
| Medium shot | Roughly waist upward; balances a person with the setting. |
| Medium close-up | Roughly chest upward; common for dialogue and reaction. |
| Close-up | Face or an important object fills much of the frame; emphasizes detail or emotion. |
| Extreme close-up / insert | A small feature or detail; directs attention to a specific clue or action. |
| Establishing shot | Introduces the location or spatial arrangement; often wide, but not defined by shot size alone. |
| Two-shot / group shot | Frames two or more subjects together to show their relationship or interaction. |
| Over-the-shoulder (OTS) | Frames one subject past another’s shoulder; connects speakers and preserves screen direction. |
| Point-of-view (POV) | Approximates a character’s or operator’s viewpoint. |
| Cutaway / reaction shot | Shows a related detail or listener; generally a shot/editing choice rather than camera movement. |

## 2. Camera angle, height, and orientation

Angle describes where the camera looks from, relative to the subject; it can change perceived scale, power, and spatial information.

- **Eye-level:** camera near the subject’s eye height; often reads as direct or neutral.
- **High angle:** looks down on the subject; reveals layout and may make the subject appear smaller.
- **Low angle:** looks up; emphasizes height, scale, or imposing architecture.
- **Overhead / top-down / bird’s-eye:** looks nearly straight down; graphic view of patterns, blocking, or geography.
- **Worm’s-eye:** very low viewpoint looking upward; exaggerates foreground scale and height.
- **Dutch / canted angle:** rolls the camera so the horizon is not level; can signal instability or stylization.
- **Profile, three-quarter, and frontal views:** orient the subject to the camera and change how expression, depth, and eyelines read.
- **Subjective vs. objective viewpoint:** places the viewer at or near a character’s viewpoint versus observing from outside it.

These are expressive conventions, not fixed emotional rules; context and performance affect interpretation.

## 3. Camera movement

Movement may come from rotating the camera in place, translating the camera through space, changing focal length, or combining these actions.

| Technique | Camera action |
|---|---|
| Pan | Rotates horizontally from a position; follows action or reveals space. |
| Tilt | Rotates vertically; follows a subject or reveals height. |
| Roll | Rotates around the lens axis; can create a canted horizon or full rotation. |
| Dolly in / out | Camera physically moves toward / away from the subject; changes perspective and spatial relationships. |
| Tracking / trucking | Camera physically travels alongside, behind, or ahead of a subject. |
| Pedestal / boom | Camera moves vertically while maintaining its general orientation. |
| Crane / jib | Camera travels on an elevated arm, often combining vertical and sweeping movement. |
| Handheld | Operator supports and moves the camera; may feel immediate or unstable. |
| Stabilized walk / gimbal / Steadicam | Stabilization reduces unwanted shake while the camera travels; each system handles movement differently. |
| Vehicle-mounted / car rig | Camera is attached to or operated from a moving vehicle. |
| Aerial / drone | Camera moves above or around a scene using an aircraft; follow local aviation and privacy rules. |
| Whip pan | Very fast pan that produces motion blur; may connect scenes or convey energy. |
| Zoom in / out | Lens focal length changes while the camera remains in place; unlike a dolly, perspective does not change in the same way. |
| Dolly zoom / “Vertigo” effect | Dolly and zoom are coordinated in opposite directions to change background scale while keeping subject size similar. |
| Static / locked-off | Camera remains fixed; staging, movement within the frame, or duration carries the shot. |

Camera motion can also be described by its path: push/pull, lateral pass, arc/orbit, follow, lead, rise/fall, reveal, or a combination. A motivated move serves attention or story; unnecessary motion can distract.

## 4. Focus, depth, and lens choices

- **Deep focus:** a broad range of distances appears acceptably sharp.
- **Shallow / selective focus:** a narrow range is sharp, separating a subject from foreground or background.
- **Rack focus / focus pull:** focus shifts between distances during a shot to redirect attention.
- **Follow focus / continuous autofocus:** focus is adjusted manually or automatically to keep a moving subject sharp.
- **Split diopter:** an auxiliary lens element allows near and far portions of the image to be focused at once, usually leaving a visible focus boundary.
- **Focus stacking:** multiple still images focused at different distances are combined to increase apparent depth of field.
- **Macro / close-up:** captures small subjects at close range; working distance, lighting, and depth of field become important.
- **Focal length:** shorter focal lengths show a wider field of view from a fixed camera position; longer focal lengths show a narrower view. Perspective is governed primarily by camera position, not focal length alone.
- **Prime vs. zoom:** a prime has a fixed focal length; a zoom varies focal length.
- **Perspective-control / tilt-shift:** lens movements can control convergence or plane of focus; used in architecture, product work, and miniature-like effects.
- **Anamorphic:** an optical system squeezes a wider scene onto the recording area, with a corresponding desqueeze in display or post-production.
- **Filters:** neutral-density filters reduce light (useful for chosen shutter/aperture in bright conditions); polarizers reduce some reflections and alter sky/water appearance; diffusion and color filters alter rendering or color.

## 5. Exposure, motion rendering, and timing

Exposure is controlled by aperture, exposure time (shutter speed or shutter angle in video), and ISO setting / sensor gain, alongside available light. Changing one can alter depth of field, motion blur, noise, or highlight detail.

- **Fast shutter / short exposure:** reduces motion blur and can freeze action; may make movement look staccato in video.
- **Slow shutter / long exposure:** records movement as blur or light trails; tripod support can keep stationary details sharp.
- **Panning with a moving subject:** follows the subject with a slower shutter so the subject may remain relatively sharp against a blurred background.
- **Exposure compensation / manual exposure:** biases or directly sets exposure when automation would render a scene too bright or dark.
- **Bracketing / HDR:** captures different exposures for selection or combination; moving subjects can cause artifacts in composites.
- **Highlight / shadow control:** expose and light with the recording medium’s dynamic range in mind; protect important highlights or preserve shadow detail as required.
- **Silhouette / backlight:** places a subject against a brighter background and exposes for the background or desired outline.
- **Frame rate:** recording more frames per second and playing them back at a lower rate creates slow motion; recording fewer frames and playing them back faster creates time-lapse or accelerated motion.
- **Time-lapse:** captures frames at intervals to condense slow change into a shorter sequence.
- **High-speed capture:** records at elevated frame rates for slowed playback; requires sufficient light, storage, and compatible equipment.
- **Flash synchronization:** ordinary, high-speed, or rear-curtain sync determines how flash is timed relative to the shutter; rear-curtain sync can place motion trails behind a flashed subject.

In video, a conventional motion cadence often pairs frame rate and exposure time (commonly expressed as shutter angle); this is a stylistic choice, not a requirement.

## 6. Composition and camera placement

- **Rule of thirds / grid placement:** places subjects near grid lines or intersections; a guide rather than a rule.
- **Centering and symmetry:** uses balance, alignment, or intentional centrality.
- **Leading lines and vanishing points:** direct the eye or reinforce depth.
- **Foreground, middle ground, and background:** layer a frame to create depth and organize visual information.
- **Frame within a frame:** uses a doorway, branches, or another boundary to contain the subject.
- **Negative space:** leaves unoccupied areas to simplify the image, create scale, or give a subject visual room.
- **Headroom, look room, and lead room:** manage space above a subject or in the direction they face or move.
- **Visual balance, contrast, and color:** distribute visual weight and separate or connect elements.
- **Screen direction and eyeline matching:** maintain spatial continuity across coverage and cuts.
- **Depth cues and perspective:** use camera position, overlap, relative scale, and focus to describe spatial relationships.
- **Dutch/canted composition:** tilts the frame deliberately; distinct from an accidental crooked horizon.

Composition is not limited to a particular grid: framing should support the subject, action, and intended reading.

## 7. Still-photography and computational techniques

- **Long exposure / light painting:** keeps the shutter open while light or subjects move; may require a tripod and remote release.
- **Intentional camera movement (ICM):** deliberately moves the camera during exposure to create abstract streaks or patterns.
- **Zoom burst:** changes focal length during a long exposure to create radiating blur.
- **Panorama:** stitches overlapping images to create a wider or taller view.
- **Multiple exposure:** combines exposures in-camera or during post-production.
- **Focus stacking:** combines focus planes for greater apparent sharpness through depth.
- **Exposure fusion / HDR:** combines captures with differing exposures to represent a wider brightness range; tone mapping affects the look.
- **Panning, freezing, and motion blur:** use shutter timing and camera movement to express speed or motion.
- **Reflection, silhouette, and backlight photography:** use scene geometry and exposure to emphasize shape or reflection.
- **Flash and ambient-light balance:** combines brief flash illumination with continuous available light.

Some are capture techniques; stitching, stacking, and compositing also depend on post-processing.

## 8. Specialized capture systems and environments

- **Underwater:** uses a sealed housing, suitable ports, and underwater lighting or ambient-light technique; water changes color and reduces visibility with depth.
- **Infrared / ultraviolet:** records wavelengths outside ordinary visible-light photography with compatible sensors and filters.
- **Thermal imaging:** uses a thermal sensor to map infrared radiation and apparent temperature; it is not ordinary visible-light photography.
- **360-degree / spherical:** captures the surroundings in all directions for reframing or immersive playback; stitching and viewer orientation matter.
- **Stereo / 3D:** uses paired viewpoints or depth sensors to represent binocular depth.
- **Aerial / drone:** captures from aircraft; comply with applicable airspace, safety, and privacy requirements.
- **Endoscopic / borescope:** uses a small camera and lens assembly to inspect confined spaces.
- **Scientific / machine vision:** uses controlled optics, illumination, filters, timing, and calibration to measure or inspect a scene rather than simply produce a pleasing image.
- **Macro, telephoto, and remote cameras:** enable close, distant, or inaccessible viewpoints; each has constraints such as depth of field, camera shake, and distortion.

## 9. Related disciplines that are often confused with camera technique

- **Lighting** (for example, high-key, low-key, three-point, soft, or hard lighting) controls illumination and contrast; it interacts with camera exposure and placement but is not itself a camera movement or framing technique.
- **Editing patterns** (for example, shot/reverse-shot, match cuts, montage, and continuity cutting) organize recorded shots after or during capture.
- **Blocking and staging** arrange performers and action relative to the camera.
- **Color grading and visual effects** alter image appearance after capture.

They are included here to clarify the boundary of the taxonomy; effective cinematography coordinates them.

## 10. Choosing a technique

Start with the subject, intended audience attention, and practical constraints. Choose framing and viewpoint to show relevant information; choose movement only when it improves the reveal, follows action, or changes perspective; choose focus and exposure for legibility and desired motion rendering. Check support, available light, lens limits, focus distance, recording format, sound needs, and location safety. Test the result on the intended display, since aspect ratio, stabilization, and playback affect the final image.
